The arithmetic nobody has put in front of you

A thousand hours is twenty-five working weeks. It is the single largest block of capacity in your business, and it is being spent by the person who holds the licence.

We do not know your hourly rate and we are not going to guess it. But you do — so multiply it out before the call. Whatever number you get is what the admin is costing you a year, and it is almost certainly more than any software or any help would cost to fix it.

And note what it is not costing you: it is not costing you customers. You have work. It is costing you the ability to take the work you already have.

What twenty hours usually turns out to be

In this trade, that block is almost always the same five things:

  • Scheduling and rescheduling — phone tag with customers to find a slot
  • Quoting — writing the same quote again in a slightly different order
  • Invoicing and chasing payment — the one everybody leaves until Sunday
  • Job records — what was fitted, what part, which machine, on which visit
  • Remembering — who is due a service, whose ice machine was struggling last time

Every one of those is a solved problem, and none of the solutions require you to hire anybody. Which of the five is actually eating your week is the first question for the call.

This is the finding that solves your actual problem

A maintenance agreement is the only kind of growth that does not need another employee, because you choose when the work happens.

  • It is scheduled. You plan it into the quiet weeks instead of reacting to whoever rings first.
  • It is the same customer. No acquisition cost, no quoting, no chasing.
  • It is recurring. Revenue that arrives whether or not anything breaks.
  • It cuts the emergencies. Serviced machines fail less, and emergencies are what wreck a week.
  • It sells the repairs anyway. You are inside the building twice a year, looking at the equipment.

For ice machines this is close to a duty of care. They are food-contact equipment in commercial kitchens, they need cleaning on a schedule, and the operator often does not know that until something goes wrong. You have eleven mentions of ice machines on your site and no mention of a service plan.

And it connects straight back to Finding 1: a contract book is exactly what a CRM is for. Who is due, when, what was done last time, and a reminder that goes out without you remembering.

Your quote page URL is misspelled. It reads get-a-qoute. Nobody will notice consciously — but it is the page where people ask you for money, and it is the one address you would ever type out or put on a van.

You have three distinct businesses and no page for any of them. Air conditioning, commercial refrigeration and ice machines are three different customers with three different problems. They are all on one homepage.

What We Would Do, In Order
  • 1. Work out which of the five admin tasks is actually eating the twenty hours. Everything else follows from that answer.
  • 2. Put a CRM in that does scheduling, quoting, invoicing and job history in one place — and set it up for you, rather than handing you a login.
  • 3. Build a maintenance agreement offer — tiers, pricing, and what is included — for ice machines first, then refrigeration.
  • 4. Automate the service reminders, so the contract book runs without you remembering it.
  • 5. Fix the website: delete the two "blank" pages, correct the quote URL, add a page each for AC, refrigeration and ice machines.
  • 6. Move email onto mjbservices.net.
  • 7. Put the licence, the twenty years and your reviews where a commercial client sees them.
  • 8. Install analytics — free — so anything after this can actually be judged.

Note the order. Items 1 to 4 give you back capacity and revenue without a single new customer. Items 5 to 8 are the website, and they matter — but they matter second, because you do not currently want more calls.
